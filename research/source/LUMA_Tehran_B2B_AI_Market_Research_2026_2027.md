# Identifying High-Potential B2B AI Markets in Tehran, Iran (2026–2027)

**Client:** LUMA  
**Scope:** Tehran Province, with emphasis on Tehran city and major commercial/industrial clusters  
**Commercial objective:** Find established, operationally complex, under-digitized companies that can justify custom AI/software projects through measurable ROI.  
**Research basis:** Recovered from the two completed deep-research runs executed on 1 September 2026. This export does **not** initiate a new research run.

# 1. Executive conclusion

The strongest B2B opportunity for LUMA in Tehran is **not “AI for tech companies.”** It is **custom operational software and AI for mid-market companies that already have scale, transactions, staff and digital records, but still coordinate important work through people, spreadsheets, PDFs, phone calls, messaging apps and disconnected software.**

The completed research converged on one dominant pattern: **mid-sized distributors/importers, manufacturers, industrial suppliers, auto-parts businesses, food/FMCG operators, medical-equipment companies, engineering contractors and logistics providers** are the best starting pool. These businesses usually have enough purchasing power and process complexity to pay for custom systems, while being less likely than fintechs, SaaS firms or major digital platforms to have strong in-house AI/product engineering teams.

**VERIFIED FACT:** The Tehran Chamber of Commerce describes itself as having more than 40,000 members/card holders, providing a large discovery universe for established commercial companies. [Tehran Chamber](https://service.tccim.ir/)  
**VERIFIED FACT:** Tehran Province’s industrial-estates authority publishes current industrial-estate statistics and information, making industrial zones a practical company-discovery channel. [Tehran ISIPO](https://tehran.isipo.ir/%D8%A7%D8%B7%D9%84%D8%A7%D8%B9-%D8%B1%D8%B3%D8%A7%D9%86%DB%8C)  
**VERIFIED FACT:** Industry associations expose useful prospect pools. For example, the human-pharmaceutical industry syndicate states that it includes 129 manufacturing members, 77 affiliated companies, 15 exporters and 8 pharmaceutical distribution companies. [Syndipharma](https://syndipharma.org/)  

The core targeting principle is the **digital-maturity paradox**:

> **Best prospect = strong business + operational complexity + usable digital information + weak/moderate digital maturity + clear ROI + limited internal AI capability.**

Very low digital maturity is bad because data, process discipline, integrations and management readiness are weak. Very high digital maturity is also often bad because the prospect already has product, data, software or AI teams and can build internally. The highest outsourcing fit sits in the middle.

The first LUMA offer should therefore be a **bounded operational pilot**, not a vague “AI transformation” program. Good entry projects have one process owner, one measurable bottleneck, limited integration dependency, human approval where needed and a payback story that management can understand.

Best first-project families:

1. **Quotation / RFQ automation** — incoming request → product/spec matching → price/stock lookup → draft quotation → approval → follow-up.
2. **Order and document intake** — email/PDF/image/message → structured order/record → validation → ERP/accounting/CRM handoff.
3. **Internal knowledge and document search** — contracts, manuals, catalogs, SOPs, policies, tender documents and prior projects.
4. **Operations reporting** — scattered Excel/ERP exports → normalized management dashboard → exception alerts → scheduled reports.
5. **Sales and customer-response copilots** — lead capture, qualification, history retrieval, response drafting, follow-up and lost-lead detection.
6. **Procurement and supplier workflows** — requests, comparison, approvals, supplier history, delivery tracking and document reconciliation.
7. **Inventory / replenishment assistance** — demand signals, slow-moving stock, stock-out risk and purchase recommendations with human review.

A credible LUMA sale should quantify value using at least one of these units: **employee-hours saved, faster quote turnaround, fewer missed leads, lower rework/error rate, avoided headcount, more orders processed per employee, lower stock-out/overstock exposure, faster collections or shorter approval cycle.**

### Why the top of the ranking is operational rather than “digital”

The top categories share four characteristics:

1. **There is an expensive human loop.** A person repeatedly reads requests, searches files or software, copies data, checks rules, creates a document, sends it, waits for approval and follows up.
2. **There is enough existing digital evidence to automate.** Price lists, Excel files, ERP exports, PDFs, emails, catalogs, invoices, work orders, historical quotes and SOPs already exist.
3. **Management can measure the result.** Quote turnaround, order-entry time, rework, customer response time, processed volume, inventory exceptions and staff hours are observable.
4. **The company often lacks the internal engineering depth to build a reliable custom system itself.** This is the outsourcing window LUMA should exploit.

# 3. Top priority segments

## 3.1 B2B distributors / importers / wholesalers

**Why first:** Best balance of accessibility, budget, measurable commercial ROI and low-to-medium implementation risk.

Typical process:

`Customer request → salesperson interprets request → checks catalog/price/stock → asks operations/warehouse → prepares quotation → manager approves → sends → follows up → manually records order/CRM`

Likely pain:

- Quotes take hours or days.
- Product naming/SKU descriptions vary between customer and internal catalog.
- Prices are spread across files or systems.
- Leads arrive through phone, email, Telegram/WhatsApp and forms.
- Salespeople repeatedly ask the same technical/product questions.
- Follow-up is inconsistent.

LUMA wedge:

**AI-assisted quotation + order-intake system** that extracts the request, maps items to catalog/SKUs, retrieves current pricing/stock, flags ambiguity, drafts the quotation and keeps a human approval step.

Measure:

- median quote turnaround time;
- quotes per salesperson/day;
- correction rate;
- missed/unanswered requests;
- quote-to-order conversion;
- time spent on product lookup.

## 3.2 Mid-market manufacturing

**Why first:** High-value workflows and strong expansion potential after one successful pilot.

Best starting workflows are not autonomous factory control. Start with **information work around production**: reporting, QA documentation, maintenance knowledge, procurement, shift handovers and management visibility.

LUMA wedge:

`shift/production data + Excel/ERP exports + incident logs → standardized daily report → exception detection → management dashboard → action owner`

Measure:

- report preparation hours;
- time to identify production exceptions;
- repeated data entry;
- maintenance resolution time;
- document retrieval time;
- rework attributable to information gaps.

## 3.3 Auto-parts manufacturers / distributors

**Why first:** Product catalogs are complex, compatibility questions are repetitive and wholesale operations generate clear sales/stock workflows.

LUMA wedge:

**Parts knowledge + cross-reference + quotation copilot.** User enters vehicle/model/OEM number/customer description; system retrieves candidate parts, compatibility, inventory and price; human confirms before sale.

Measure: search time, wrong-part returns, quote time, orders handled per salesperson.

## 3.4 Food / FMCG manufacturers and distributors

**Why first:** High-frequency orders and multi-layer distribution make small per-order savings compound quickly.

LUMA wedge:

**Order intake + distributor reporting + stock/replenishment exception system.** Do not begin with black-box demand forecasting. First normalize current sales/distributor data and surface actionable exceptions.

Measure: order-entry time, stock-out incidents, manual report hours, distributor response time, sales-rep admin time.

## 3.5 Medical-equipment distributors/importers

**Why first:** High-value technical selling, tenders, complex catalogs and document-heavy regulatory/customer processes.

**VERIFIED FACT:** The completed research identified public member/company directories for medical-equipment associations, which makes prospect discovery practical. [AMEDAL](https://www.amedal.co/) and [medical/lab supplier association](https://imedlabco.ir/)

LUMA wedge:

**Technical RFQ/tender assistant**: parse tender/specification → map requirements to product portfolio → retrieve evidence from manuals/catalogs → identify gaps → draft compliant response and quotation for human review.

Measure: tender-response hours, spec-search time, missed requirements, quote turnaround.

## 3.6 Engineering / EPC contractors

**Why first:** Large document volumes and expensive professional time. A useful assistant can save hours without touching high-risk engineering decisions.

LUMA wedge:

- tender document parsing;
- contract clause search;
- prior-project knowledge retrieval;
- correspondence/submittal classification;
- automated weekly project-report compilation.

Measure: tender review hours, time locating precedent, document-routing time, reporting hours.

## 3.7 Logistics / freight forwarding

**Why first:** Operations teams process status requests, shipping documents, rate requests and exceptions continuously.

**VERIFIED FACT:** The International Transport Companies Association of Iran and other transport associations expose member information suitable for company discovery. [ITCAI](https://itcai.ir/) and [rail transport association](https://rtcguild.ir/)

LUMA wedge:

**Rate request + shipment-document + status workflow** integrated around current systems rather than replacing the TMS/ERP.

Measure: response time, documents processed per operator, status-call volume, exception-resolution time.

## 3.8 Printing / packaging

**Why first:** Strongly spec-driven B2B quoting and production handoffs; moderate tech maturity; easier to demonstrate than many regulated sectors.

LUMA wedge:

**Job intake / estimation assistant** that structures customer specs, detects missing information, prepares a quote package, generates production handoff data and tracks approval.

Measure: quote time, revision cycles, jobs delayed by missing specs, salesperson/prepress admin time.

# 4. Segments to avoid

## 4.1 Major software, SaaS, fintech, marketplace and digitally native companies

They look attractive because they understand AI, but often have exactly the capability LUMA is selling: software engineers, product managers, data teams, cloud infrastructure and sometimes AI/ML staff. They may buy APIs or specialist infrastructure, but they are weak targets for broad outsourced custom-AI projects.

**Disqualifier:** visible engineering organization, AI/ML hiring, proprietary AI products, developer platform, active data-science team or major internal digital-transformation unit.

## 4.2 Micro-businesses and small traditional shops

They may have severe inefficiency but lack scale, structured data, management bandwidth and software budget. Avoid unless the same solution can be standardized across hundreds of similar businesses.

## 4.3 Banks, telecoms and very large regulated enterprises as first-wave targets

They have money and workflows but also security reviews, procurement cycles, vendor registration, internal IT, architecture standards and political/organizational complexity. They are expansion targets after LUMA has strong enterprise references, not the fastest route to initial B2B traction.

## 4.4 Core clinical / diagnostic AI

Healthcare administration is attractive; diagnosis, treatment recommendations and autonomous interpretation of medical results carry much higher regulatory, safety and liability risk. LUMA should initially stay on the administrative, knowledge-management and workflow side.

## 4.5 Government and quasi-government organizations with long tenders

Potential contract size can be high, but sales-cycle duration and procurement uncertainty can destroy cash efficiency for a small vendor. Only pursue when a sponsor, tender fit and payment path are already visible.

## 4.6 Highly bespoke professional work with low repetition

Small architecture studios, boutique consulting firms, specialist legal practices and similar businesses may like AI, but if each engagement is unique and headcount is low, ROI from custom workflow software may be weaker than simply using existing tools.

# 5. Ideal Customer Profiles

## ICP A — Established B2B distributor/importer

- **Industry:** industrial products, equipment, electronics, chemicals, building materials, medical equipment, auto parts, FMCG distribution.
- **Employees:** roughly 50–500.
- **Scale signals:** warehouse, several salespeople, broad catalog, multiple brands, national/regional distribution, dealer network or repeat corporate buyers.
- **Digital maturity:** ERP/accounting/Excel exists, but CRM/workflow integration is weak; website is informational/catalog-oriented rather than deeply transactional.
- **Likely software:** accounting/ERP, Excel, messaging apps, email, maybe basic CRM.
- **Pain:** quoting, order entry, stock/price lookup, lead follow-up, product knowledge, supplier documents.
- **Champion:** Commercial/Sales Director or Operations Director.
- **Authority:** CEO/Managing Director in owner-led firms; COO/Commercial Director in larger firms.
- **Budget holder:** CEO/MD, commercial or IT budget depending on structure.
- **Trigger events:** rapid catalog growth, new warehouse/branch, sales hiring, ERP migration, export/import complexity, complaints about response speed, headcount pressure.
- **Warning signs:** fewer than ~10 office staff; no digital price/catalog data; one-person sales function; strong internal product engineering team.

## ICP B — Mid-market manufacturer

- **Employees:** roughly 80–1,000.
- **Scale signals:** factory + head office; QA/QC; maintenance; procurement; warehouse; certifications; multi-shift operations.
- **Digital maturity:** ERP/accounting or production software exists but reporting remains Excel/PDF/manual.
- **Pain:** fragmented reports, maintenance knowledge, quality documents, production visibility, procurement approvals.
- **Champion:** COO, Factory Manager, Operations Director, QA Manager for narrow projects.
- **Authority/budget:** MD/CEO/COO; IT is technical gatekeeper.
- **Trigger:** expansion, new production line, audit/certification pressure, rising headcount, ERP project, downtime/rework problem.
- **Warning:** advanced MES/data platform + large software/data team; highly proprietary OT environment with no safe integration path.

## ICP C — Technical B2B seller / industrial equipment supplier

- **Employees:** 20–250.
- **Traits:** engineers support sales; long catalogs/manuals; formal quotations; tender participation; imported components.
- **Pain:** technical matching, proposal writing, manual search, inconsistent sales knowledge.
- **Champion:** Sales/Commercial Director.
- **Best pilot:** technical-sales knowledge + proposal/quote copilot.
- **Disqualifier:** very low quote volume or fully standardized e-commerce sales.

## ICP D — Engineering/EPC contractor

- **Employees:** 50–1,000.
- **Traits:** multiple projects, tendering, contracts, engineering correspondence and document control.
- **Pain:** information retrieval, tender parsing, progress/report compilation, clause/requirement tracking.
- **Champion:** PMO/Project Controls/Commercial/Contracts Director.
- **Technical gatekeeper:** IT/document-control manager.
- **Best pilot:** tender/contract/project knowledge assistant with source citations and access control.
- **Disqualifier:** client data cannot leave a closed environment and LUMA cannot deploy within accepted infrastructure.

## ICP E — Logistics/freight forwarder

- **Employees:** 30–500.
- **Traits:** high daily shipment communication; operations/customer-service teams; many shipping documents.
- **Pain:** rate requests, status calls, document classification, exception handling.
- **Champion:** Operations Director.
- **Best pilot:** shipment document + status + rate-response workflow.
- **Disqualifier:** tiny fleet/agency with low B2B volume.

## ICP F — Medical-equipment distributor

- **Employees:** 30–300.
- **Traits:** technical catalog; tender business; hospital/lab buyers; after-sales service.
- **Pain:** tender documents, technical matching, quotations, manuals, service history.
- **Champion:** Commercial Director; Technical/Regulatory leader as co-champion.
- **Best pilot:** tender/RFQ assistant with strict evidence retrieval.
- **Disqualifier:** inadequate document control, highly sensitive data requirement with no deployment/security fit.

## ICP G — Multi-branch operator

- **Employees:** 100–1,000+.
- **Traits:** central operations and many branches/stores/clinics/labs/service points.
- **Pain:** branch reporting, incident escalation, repeated support, inventory/transfer requests.
- **Champion:** COO/Operations Director.
- **Best pilot:** branch operations dashboard + structured incident/reporting workflow.
- **Disqualifier:** already operates a mature, integrated enterprise platform with strong internal product team.

### What LUMA should avoid automating first

- irreversible financial approval;
- autonomous purchasing above thresholds;
- clinical diagnosis;
- safety-critical industrial control;
- legal conclusions without human review;
- any process where source data is inaccessible or untrustworthy.

Use **human-in-the-loop** design: AI prepares, extracts, recommends, prioritizes or drafts; authorized staff confirm consequential decisions.

### Contact sequencing

For owner-led Iranian mid-market companies, **CEO/Managing Director access can be valuable**, but outreach is stronger when it names a concrete process owned by a director below the CEO. The recommended path is:

1. identify the **problem owner**;
2. show a workflow-specific hypothesis;
3. ask for a short process walkthrough;
4. validate data/integration feasibility with IT;
5. present business case to the authority/budget holder.

Do not begin by asking the IT manager to “buy AI.” IT is frequently a gatekeeper rather than the person who experiences the operational pain.

# 8. Company scoring system

## 8.1 0–100 prospect qualification formula

Score each factor from **0 to 5**, then multiply by its weight.

| Factor | Weight | Meaning of a high factor score |
|---|---:|---|
| Company strength / purchasing power | 12 | Established, credible, able to fund a custom project |
| Operational complexity | 12 | Multiple teams, locations, products, transactions or approvals |
| Repetitive workflow density | 12 | Many repeated human steps suitable for automation |
| Potential AI ROI | 15 | Clear labor, speed, revenue, error or working-capital benefit |
| Usable digital information | 8 | Relevant data already exists in files/systems/messages/databases |
| Digital-maturity sweet spot | 10 | Mature enough to integrate, not so mature that they build internally |
| Decision-maker accessibility | 7 | Named/visible reachable owners or directors |
| Expected project size | 7 | Pilot can expand into valuable departmental/multi-team system |
| Outsourcing fit / low internal AI capability | 7 | Limited evidence of strong in-house AI/product engineering |
| Implementation feasibility | 5 | Data/integration/security path is practical |
| Sales-cycle ease | 3 | Procurement and approval are manageable |
| Regulatory feasibility | 2 | Project can be delivered without disproportionate legal/compliance burden |
| **Total** | **100** |  |

Formula:

```text
Opportunity Score = Σ (factor_score / 5 × factor_weight)
```

## 8.2 Non-monotonic digital maturity

First rate **observed digital maturity M** from 0–5:

- 0 = mostly paper/phone; almost no usable digital process
- 1 = basic website/accounting; fragmented digital records
- 2 = some ERP/accounting/CRM/Excel; many manual handoffs
- 3 = functional core systems but weak integration/automation — **ideal**
- 4 = strong integrated systems, analytics, mature IT department
- 5 = digital-native / strong product-data-engineering capability

Convert M to the scoring factor used above:

| Observed maturity M | Digital-maturity sweet-spot factor (0–5) |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0.5 |
| 1 | 1.5 |
| 2 | 4.0 |
| 3 | **5.0** |
| 4 | 3.0 |
| 5 | 1.0 |

This explicitly penalizes both extremes.

## 8.3 Score interpretation

- **85–100 — Tier A:** immediate research + personalized outreach. Strong likelihood of a valuable custom workflow project.
- **75–84 — Tier B:** high priority; validate one missing variable before outreach.
- **65–74 — Tier C:** conditional; pursue only with a clear trigger or obvious workflow.
- **50–64 — Tier D:** weak; nurture or skip unless inbound.
- **Below 50 — Disqualify:** insufficient scale, readiness, accessibility or outsourcing fit.

## 8.4 Hard disqualification overrides

Even a high calculated score should be rejected or manually reviewed if:

- company appears inactive/closed;
- no credible contact or legal identity can be confirmed;
- data needed for the proposed workflow does not exist or cannot legally/securely be used;
- internal AI/software team is clearly capable of building the same system;
- expected payback is weak;
- project requires replacing a mission-critical core system before value can be shown;
- payment/procurement risk is unacceptable.

## 8.5 ROI model for proposals

Use a conservative annualized-value model:

```text
Annual Value =
  labor hours saved × loaded hourly cost
+ avoided rework/errors
+ avoided incremental hires
+ gross profit from additional throughput/conversion
+ inventory / working-capital benefit
+ faster collection / lower delay cost
```

Then:

```text
Payback months = Project Cost / Conservative Monthly Validated Benefit
```

Commercial gate:

- **< 12 months:** strong
- **12–18 months:** acceptable when strategic or expandable
- **> 18 months:** weak for an initial LUMA pilot unless compliance/risk reduction is compelling

Do not sell “AI accuracy.” Sell the validated business delta.

# 9. Public qualification signals

The future qualification agent should distinguish **evidence** from **inference**. Every detected signal should record source URL, capture date and confidence.

## 9.1 Company strength signals

**Positive public signals:**

- multiple branches, factories, warehouses or offices;
- long operating history / older legal registration;
- named major corporate customers or projects;
- large product/SKU catalog;
- national distribution/dealer network;
- certifications and quality standards;
- active hiring across operations/sales/finance/production;
- membership in recognized industry association;
- participation in major exhibitions;
- public financial filings for listed companies;
- recent expansion, facility opening, production line or new branch;
- professional corporate website and complete legal/contact details.

**Useful sources:** Rasmio/legal data, official website, association membership, job boards, Codal for public companies, maps, exhibition/member lists.

## 9.2 Operational-complexity signals

- many product categories/brands;
- distributor/dealer network;
- several service lines;
- tender/projects page;
- after-sales/service network;
- multiple departments shown on website/job ads;
- several geographic locations;
- downloadable catalogs/price lists/spec sheets;
- forms for quotation/service/representation;
- logistics/warehouse/factory footprint.

## 9.3 Moderate / under-digitized maturity signals

These are **not automatically negative**. In a strong company, they can be sales signals:

- website primarily brochure/catalog with phone-based conversion;
- quote request requires manual call/email follow-up;
- important information only available as PDFs;
- downloadable Excel/PDF forms;
- no customer/dealer portal;
- no visible self-service order/status system;
- several phone numbers for routine functions;
- job ads for high volumes of data entry, administrative coordination or reporting;
- repeated “contact sales for price/availability” across large catalog;
- fragmented social/channel communication;
- old or weak search across a large document catalog.

## 9.4 Usable-data signals

- product catalog with SKU/specification data;
- PDF manuals, technical datasheets, price lists;
- portal/login implying structured backend data;
- ERP/CRM/BI names in job listings;
- downloadable reports/forms;
- online order or inquiry forms;
- customer-service/ticket infrastructure;
- large archive of projects/news/products that can seed a knowledge system.

## 9.5 Negative internal-tech signals

- active hiring for ML Engineer, Data Scientist, AI Engineer, LLM Engineer, MLOps;
- large software/product/engineering organization;
- developer documentation or public API platform as a core product;
- proprietary AI features marketed to customers;
- internal data/AI lab;
- mature cloud/devops/platform engineering organization;
- company itself sells software, AI, automation or digital-transformation services.

## 9.6 Confidence model

For every field or inference, store:

- **Evidence class:** Verified / Strong inference / Weak inference / Unknown.
- **Confidence:** 0.00–1.00.
- **Source quality:** Primary / Association / Registry / Reputable media / Directory / Social / Other.
- **Freshness:** source publication/update date when available + research capture date.

A workflow pain should **not** be stated as fact merely because it is common in the industry. Example:

```text
Verified: website lists 4,000+ products and asks customers to contact sales for quotation.
Inference: sales team likely spends substantial time on product lookup and quote preparation.
Unknown: whether prices/stock are already integrated internally.
Discovery question: “How do incoming RFQs currently become approved quotations?”
```

That is the standard the proposal agent should follow.

# 12. Recommended acquisition strategy

## Stage 1 — Market research and vertical definition

Start with **6–8 vertical batches**, not the whole Tehran economy. Recommended initial batches:

1. distributors/importers;
2. industrial manufacturers;
3. auto parts;
4. food/FMCG distribution/manufacturing;
5. medical equipment;
6. engineering/EPC;
7. logistics;
8. printing/packaging.

For each batch, define 3–5 workflow hypotheses before collecting companies. This prevents the database from becoming a generic directory.

## Stage 2 — Company discovery

Collect companies from high-signal vertical sources: TCCIM, ISIPO, industry associations, maps, exhibitions and member directories. Capture source and discovery reason immediately.

Minimum discovery record:

`name + website/domain + industry + Tehran location + source URL + one strength signal`

## Stage 3 — Automated qualification

Run fast public enrichment and calculate the preliminary 0–100 score.

Automatic filters:

- active website/domain;
- Tehran HQ/operation;
- minimum scale signal;
- industry match;
- no obvious AI/software-vendor disqualifier;
- enough public data for a credible next step.

Do **not** spend expensive AI research on every discovered company. First eliminate the obvious weak prospects.

## Stage 4 — Website and public-footprint analysis

For Tier A/B candidates, analyze:

- products/services and customer type;
- catalog size/complexity;
- quote/order/service workflow visible online;
- PDFs/forms/downloads;
- branch/factory/warehouse footprint;
- job listings and department structure;
- portal/self-service maturity;
- tech/AI hiring;
- current software clues;
- named directors/decision makers;
- recent expansion/news/triggers.

Output must cite each observed fact.

## Stage 5 — AI opportunity detection

The agent maps evidence to a workflow library. It should **not** start from “what AI can we sell?” It should start from:

`observable business operation → likely manual handoff → cost/delay/error → data available → narrow automatable step`

Each company gets no more than **1–3 primary opportunity hypotheses**. More ideas reduce credibility.

## Stage 6 — Personalized proposal hypothesis

Create a one-page hypothesis, not a full commercial proposal:

- what LUMA observed;
- what is inferred;
- why the process may be costly;
- proposed pilot;
- what data would be needed;
- success metric;
- one discovery question.

Example:

> “Your site exposes a broad technical catalog and routes quotation requests to sales. We would not assume your internal quoting is manual, but this pattern often creates repeated product/spec lookup. A 2–3 week discovery/pilot could test whether incoming RFQs can be mapped to your catalog and converted into draft quotations with human approval. Success would be measured by quote preparation time and correction rate.”

## Stage 7 — Human review

A human must verify:

- company is genuinely attractive;
- public evidence supports the personalization;
- no sensitive/incorrect inference is stated as fact;
- contact is appropriate;
- proposed workflow is realistic;
- the message is concise and commercially credible.

## Stage 8 — Outreach

Lead with the **specific workflow and measurable outcome**, not AI technology.

Bad:

> “LUMA provides advanced AI solutions that can transform your business.”

Good:

> “We are looking at how industrial distributors handle incoming RFQs. Your product range suggests quote preparation may involve repeated catalog, price and stock checks. We build internal tools that turn an incoming request into a structured draft quote for sales approval, without replacing the ERP. If this is a real bottleneck for your team, the first step is a short process walkthrough.”

## Stage 9 — Discovery and baseline

Before pricing, obtain baseline numbers:

- requests/orders/tickets per day/month;
- average handling time;
- people involved;
- current error/rework rate;
- systems/files used;
- approval steps;
- data accessibility;
- security constraints;
- current bottleneck owner.

Without baseline data, ROI claims are speculation.

## Stage 10 — Pilot design

Best pilot characteristics:

- one department;
- one workflow;
- 2–8 weeks depending on integration complexity;
- historical/sample data available;
- clear human approval point;
- measurable baseline and success metric;
- no core-system replacement;
- expansion path if successful.

## Stage 11 — Land and expand

A successful quotation assistant can expand into order intake, CRM follow-up, product knowledge and management reporting. A manufacturing reporting pilot can expand into maintenance, quality and procurement. The economic model should favor **repeatable workflow modules** across companies rather than one-off bespoke code with no reuse.
